Reservoir sampling: a fair sample from a simulation you cannot store

By the end of this lesson you can keep a fair random sample of k records from a simulation's output, in one pass and fixed memory, without knowing how many records the run will produce.

Objective O1: sample a stream of unknown length with reservoir sampling About 10 minutes First lesson in this workspace
Why this matters for you

Simulations often produce more events than you can store, and you rarely know in advance how many there will be. Reservoir sampling gives you a small sample you can trust, so what you inspect or average represents the whole run.

      Uniform sampling from a stream (Algorithm R) 5 min

      Learning goals
      • Apply Algorithm R to keep k records from a stream of unknown length.
      • Show why every record ends up in the sample with probability k/n.
      What you need to know first
      • A uniform random integer from 1 to i: each value equally likely.
      • The chance that several independent events all happen is the product of their chances.

      The general idea

      A stream is data you see once, in order, without being able to store it all or know its length in advance. A reservoir is a fixed array of k slots holding the current sample. Reservoir sampling keeps that array so that, at every moment, it is a simple random sample of the records seen so far, meaning every set of k records is equally likely (Wikipedia: Reservoir sampling).

      The basic method, Algorithm R, is credited by Vitter to Alan Waterman; Vitter's 1985 paper then gave faster variants that skip records (Vitter, "Random Sampling with a Reservoir", ACM TOMS 1985). Algorithm R draws one random number per record, so it takes time proportional to n and memory proportional to k.

      Why it is fair: record i enters with probability k/i. A later record m then evicts it only if m is accepted (k/m) and lands on its slot (1/k), so record i survives step m with probability (m − 1)/m. Multiplying over m = i+1 to n, the fractions cancel to i/n, and k/i × i/n = k/n for every record (Princeton COS 493, Lecture 19).

      Important steps

      • Put the first k records straight into the reservoir.
      • For record i > k, draw j uniformly from 1 to i, including both ends.
      • If j ≤ k, overwrite slot j with record i; otherwise discard record i.
      • When the stream ends, the reservoir is your sample.
      import random
def reservoir(stream, k):
    R = []
    for i, x in enumerate(stream, start=1):
        if i <= k:
            R.append(x)
        else:
            j = random.randint(1, i)   # 1..i inclusive
            if j <= k:
                R[j - 1] = x
    return R

      Worked example: sampling patients from an emergency-room simulation

      The case

      A discrete-event simulation of an emergency room runs until its queue stabilizes, producing one record per patient. A run can produce millions of records, and you do not know how many before it stops. You want k = 2 patient records to inspect by hand; here the run happens to produce 5.

      Why this idea applies here: you cannot store every record, and you cannot use a fixed chance like k/n, because n is unknown until the run ends.

      1. P1 and P2 fill the reservoir: [P1, P2].
      2. P3 arrives: draw j from 1 to 3 and get 2. Since 2 ≤ 2, P3 replaces slot 2: [P1, P3].
      3. P4 arrives: draw j from 1 to 4 and get 4. Since 4 > 2, discard P4.
      4. P5 arrives: draw j from 1 to 5 and get 1. P5 replaces slot 1: [P5, P3].

      The result: the sample is [P5, P3]. Over many runs, each of the five patients appears with probability 2/5, whether it arrived early or late.

      Common Mix-Ups

      Keeping each new record with a fixed chance

      • Right: the chance falls as k/i, so later records are less likely to enter, and earlier ones face fewer evictions in total.
      • Wrong: "keep each new record with probability 1/2" or "with probability k/n".
      • Why: a fixed 1/2 makes the sample almost all late records, which biases a simulation toward its end state; k/n needs the total, which you do not have.

      Off by one in the random range

      • Right: j ranges over 1 to i inclusive, with i the index of the record just arrived.
      • Wrong: drawing from 1 to i − 1, or from 0 to i when the code counts from 0.
      • Why: either shifts every probability, so the sample is no longer uniform, and no error is ever raised.

        Weighted sampling: choosing one light in a ray tracer 3 min

        Learning goals
        • Apply the one-item weighted reservoir update with a running sum of weights.
        • Predict each candidate's final chance from its weight.
        What you need to know first
        • Algorithm R and why its fractions cancel, from Section 1.

        The general idea

        Sometimes you want a candidate chosen in proportion to a weight, a non-negative number saying how much it matters. The one-item version keeps a stored sample and a running sum w_sum: when a candidate of weight w arrives, add w to w_sum, then replace the stored sample with probability w / w_sum. The method goes back to Chao (1982) and needs no stream length in advance (Weighted Reservoir Sampling With Replacement from Data Streams).

        The proof is Section 1's with weights: the chance of entering, w_i / S_i, times the chance of surviving each later step, S_(m−1) / S_m, cancels to w_i / S_n, the item's share of the total weight.

        Real-time ray tracing, a simulation of light, uses this. ReSTIR keeps one small reservoir per pixel to choose which light to sample, rendering scenes with up to 3.4 million emissive triangles in under 50 ms per frame (Bitterli et al., 2020; update rule as in Decorrelating ReSTIR Samplers via MCMC Mutations).

        Important steps

        • Start with no sample and w_sum = 0.
        • For each candidate, add its weight w to w_sum.
        • Draw u uniformly in [0, 1); if u < w / w_sum, store this candidate.
        • Keep w_sum with the sample: later steps need it to weight the estimate and to merge reservoirs.

        Worked example: one pixel, four candidate lights

        The case

        A pixel on a floor is lit by four lamps with estimated contributions 6, 2, 1 and 3. Tracing a shadow ray to every lamp is too expensive, so the renderer traces one, chosen in proportion to its contribution.

        Why this idea applies here: candidates are generated one at a time on the GPU, and there is room for one sample and one sum per pixel, not a list.

        1. L1, w = 6: w_sum = 6, chance 6/6 = 1, store L1.
        2. L2, w = 2: w_sum = 8, chance 2/8 = 0.25; u = 0.71, keep L1.
        3. L3, w = 1: w_sum = 9, chance 1/9 ≈ 0.11; u = 0.05, store L3.
        4. L4, w = 3: w_sum = 12, chance 3/12 = 0.25; u = 0.60, keep L3.

        The result: this time the dim L3 was picked, which is allowed. Across many frames, L1 wins 6/12 = 50% of the time, L2 17%, L3 8% and L4 25%.

        Common Mix-Ups

        "The heaviest candidate always wins"

        • Right: each candidate wins with probability equal to its share of the total weight.
        • Wrong: keeping the maximum weight, or replacing only when w beats the stored weight.
        • Why: always picking L1 would make the renderer ignore L4 entirely, so its light would be missing from the image instead of averaged in.

          Main points 1 min

          • Reservoir sampling keeps a fair sample of k items from a stream of unknown length, in one pass and k slots of memory.
          • Algorithm R: keep the first k; record i enters with probability k/i and replaces a uniformly random slot.
          • Every record ends in the sample with probability k/n, because the survival fractions cancel.
          • Draw j from 1 to i inclusive; a fixed acceptance chance or an off-by-one range biases the sample silently.
          • Weighted, one item: add w to a running sum, replace with probability w / w_sum; each item wins with its share of the total weight.
          • Ray tracers such as ReSTIR use weighted reservoirs to choose one light per pixel among millions.
